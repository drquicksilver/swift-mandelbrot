## Summary

This is a strong set. The rotation design is the right one — the angle applies to Double
offsets from the screen centre, so precision is untouched at any depth and tiles stay
axis-aligned, which means rotation invalidates no cached work. The tests are unusually
good: an independent CPU oracle that applies rotation itself, a companion checked against
the mathematics rather than against itself, and navigation driven frame by frame at two
refresh rates.

Two findings are worth acting on before 2.9. The movie path ignores the observed ceiling
that 2.3 had just introduced, so every keyframe pays the depth estimate's overshoot —
which is most of the 439 s in the recorded 1e30 descent, and is cheaper to fix than
either follow-up already noted. And the movie's tile store asks for 512 MiB
unconditionally, on a platform whose own viewer budget is 150 MiB, from a sheet the phone
can open. Three low findings follow.

## Good

- **Rotation is applied where it costs nothing.** `Viewport.angle` turns Double offsets
  from the screen centre (`viewOffset`/`planeOffset`), so deep precision is unaffected,
  and tiles stay axis-aligned in the plane, so rotating invalidates no tile. Every
  conversion — `preciseComplex`, both `screen(for:)`, `complex(at:)`, `pan`, `rotate` —
  goes through the same pair of helpers, so zoom anchors and rectangle-zoom inherit
  rotation rather than each needing their own fix.
- **The coverage arithmetic is honest.** `Viewport.coverage(size:)` returns the rotated
  bounding box's half-extents; at 45° on 16:9 it works out at about 2.2× the tiles, which
  is what the commit message claims. `TileBounds.intersects` grows its radius by
  |cos|+|sin| to match, and `TileProjection` rotates tile centres on screen while the
  vertex shader turns each quad about its own centre in point space — the two halves that
  have to agree, and do.
- **The rotated golden is checked against an independent oracle.** `tests/product_reference.py`
  applies the rotation itself, so the fixture tests the renderer rather than comparing the
  renderer with itself. That is the strongest form available here.
- **Navigation is tested as the display drives it.** `checkRotationAndBounds` steps
  `advanceMotion` frame by frame: the 10° twist threshold, the ±3° snap, the compass,
  rotation inertia, the pan spring settling identically at 60 and 120 Hz, the zoom-out
  spring, and the precision limit bouncing with velocity still negative rather than
  resting dead against the cap.
- **The companion is checked against the mathematics.** For c = 0 every sampled point
  inside the unit circle is capped and every point outside escapes; for c = −1 the
  critical point never escapes and a far corner does; plus a check that changing c changes
  the picture at all, which is what catches a stuck cache.
- **`ZoomPath` is real geometry with real tests.** `zoomPathKeepsTheDestinationStill`
  covers every property the plan claims — the keyframe chain and its spacing, monotone
  convergence of the destination with it always on screen, no end-lurch, exact start and
  end views, easing versus linear, fixed versus automatic depth, palette cycling, and the
  refusal of a path with nowhere to go — and `zoomPathStaysPreciseAtExtremeDepth` runs the
  338-keyframe 1e100 descent and lands within a hundredth of a pixel.
- **The movie is verified end to end.** `checkZoomMovie` reads the file back with
  AVAssetReader for frame count and timing, and compares the first, middle and last frames
  against direct renders — the middle one exercising the affine map and cross-fade that the
  end frames do not (3.2/255, against 9.3/255 through HEVC).
- **The performance record is candid.** `Performance.md` records the 439 s descent rather
  than the flattering 720p number, and names the two follow-ups it points at.
- **`ab2333a` is a good catch.** A view left outside its bounds while the companion held
  the main area reported itself animating every frame, so the display never idled. It came
  with a test.

## Findings

### 1. Movie keyframes ignore the observed ceiling — medium *(reasoned)*

`ZoomPath.iterations(at:)`:

```swift
if let limit = end.iterations { return limit }
return IterationPolicy.estimate(logScale: level)
```

That is the depth-only estimate which 2.3 documents as overshooting by about 100× at
c = i, and which the observed ceiling exists to remove. `MovieRenderer` passes it straight
into `tiles.update`, and nothing in the movie path observes anything: `grep` for
`ceiling`, `observe` or `visibleMaximumEscaped` across `MovieRenderer.swift` and
`ZoomPath.swift` returns only that one `estimate` call.

The information is already there. Each keyframe renders a full `TileStore` to completion
and waits for `waitUntilReady`, at which point `tiles.visibleMaximumEscaped` is exactly
what `ExplorerModel.observeDepth` consumes in the viewer. `Performance.md` attributes
essentially all of a movie's time to keyframe rendering, with "interior pixels each run to
the iteration limit" — so the limit is the cost, and it is currently the unbounded
estimate at every level of a descent.

This matters more than it does in the viewer. The viewer overshoots for one settle and
then corrects; a movie pays the overshoot on every one of hundreds of keyframes.

**Resolution.** Carry an `IterationPolicy.Ceiling` across the keyframe loop: observe after
each `waitUntilReady`, and feed `IterationPolicy.target(logScale:multiplier:ceiling:)` into
the next keyframe. The ceiling already models growth with depth at the estimate's slope,
which fits a descent that moves one level at a time.

Be more conservative here than in the viewer, though: an under-iterated keyframe is baked
into the file, where the viewer would simply refine. I would keep the 2× margin, re-derive
from the estimate every N levels rather than trusting a single observation for a whole
descent, and never let the ceiling apply to the destination keyframe, which the user is
most likely to inspect. Worth measuring against the recorded 242 s (1e12) and 439 s (1e30)
runs; I would expect it to beat both recorded follow-ups for effort.

### 2. The movie store asks for 512 MiB, on a phone too — medium *(reasoned)*

`MovieRenderer.render`:

```swift
let tiles = store ?? TileStore(budgetBytes: 512 * 1024 * 1024)
```

There is no platform conditional, and the movie sheet is reachable on iOS: `ContentView`
puts a "Movie" button in the `#if os(iOS)` overlay and presents
`.sheet(isPresented: $model.showMovie)` unconditionally. `TileStore`'s own default is
150 MiB on iOS for good reason, so a phone render asks for 3.4× the whole viewer budget
*in addition to* the live viewer store, plus two keyframe textures (33 MB each at the 4K
setting `MovieSettings.resolutions` offers) and AVAssetWriter's pixel-buffer pool.

**Resolution.** Give the movie store the same platform default the viewer uses, or a
fraction of it; cap the offered resolutions on iOS; and consider suspending the viewer's
store (`cancel()`) for the duration of a render, since its contents are not on screen
behind the sheet anyway.

### 3. The companion's iteration cap is doubled up and undocumented — low

`JuliaCanvas` passes `min(model.iterations, 4000)`; `JuliaRenderer.update` then clamps
again with `max(32, min(iterations, 20_000))`. The outer cap always wins, so the 20,000 is
dead, and the effective limit — 4,000 — is the thing that governs companion quality when
the main view is deep. The plan's 2.7 text documents the 2^26 zoom limit and the 2.2 MP
resolution cap but not this.

Separately, `gpu.julia` is a single unbatched dispatch, where the tile path deliberately
targets 1 ms batches. At the 2.2 MP cap and 4,000 iterations over an interior-heavy Julia
set, a swapped full-screen companion on a phone is a long command buffer with no yield.

**Resolution.** State the cap once, in `JuliaRenderer`, and record it in the plan; if a
phone shows a hitch on swap, batch the dispatch the way the tile worker does.

### 4. Dead code on a per-frame path in `ZoomPath.viewport(at:)` — low

```swift
var view = try Location(viewport: Viewport(), iterations: nil).viewport()
let centre = centre(at: level)
view = Viewport(center: centre.point, scale: pow(2, min(level, 1023)))
```

The first line is overwritten immediately, yet it constructs a `Location`, parses decimal
strings back into a `Viewport`, and can throw — on a function called once per keyframe and
once per frame. Delete it and make the binding a plain `var view = Viewport(...)`.

### 5. The rotated golden's tolerance is six times its measured error — low

`tests/fixtures/product/manifest.json` allows `maxMismatch: 0.02` for `rotated-centres`,
which measures 0.34%; the unrotated `seahorse-centres` allows 0.01 for a near-identical
0.33%. A rotation regression would have to be six times worse than today's agreement
before the fixture noticed.

**Resolution.** Tighten to 0.01, matching the unrotated fixture it was calibrated against.
